When complete documentation creates false confidence

What the Kew Recreation Centre collapse teaches about quality assurance for welded steel structures

Andreas Hardt, IWE1 August 2026

A complete QA package can create confidence without confirming the actual fabrication. The collapse of a steel structure in Melbourne shows how dangerous the gap between documentation and manufacture can become. The decisive question is not whether documents exist, but whether they were technically reviewed, tied to the actual component and consistently verified against the work performed.

In October 2022, a welded connection in a truss girder failed during construction of the Kew Recreation Centre in Melbourne, Australia. A large part of the partially erected steel structure collapsed. Because the failure occurred outside working hours, nobody was injured.

The girder had been fabricated in China. WorkSafe Victoria later reported that a substantial QA package existed, but no competent person had provided written confirmation before erection that the documentation matched the actual fabrication. The required review included welding procedures, welder qualifications, visual and ultrasonic inspection and consistency of inspection reports with drawings and fabrication requirements. The investigation found that the girder had not been fabricated in accordance with those requirements.

Completeness is not conformity

A document is not automatically a valid quality record. A WPS may be formally valid but still fail to cover the material, thickness range, joint type or welding position of the actual connection. A welder qualification only proves suitability when its range covers the work performed.

The same applies to inspection reports. A VT or UT report marked “acceptable” is meaningful only when the component and weld are uniquely identified, the required inspection extent and instruction are known, the acceptance criteria are stated and all deviations have been closed.

Drawing and fabrication requirements → WPS and procedure qualification → personnel qualification → actual fabrication → inspection planning and inspection → inspection report → closure of deviations → erection release

Three levels must be distinguished. Formal review checks whether required documents exist and remain valid. Technical review checks whether their scope and content fit the actual connection. Verification at the component confirms that the work was genuinely fabricated and inspected in accordance with those documents. Only all three levels together form a robust demonstration of conformity.

The component must remain at the centre

At each interface, the specified and actual condition must be compared for the specific component. For supplied or imported structures, review must not stop at a document checklist. The evidence must be traceable to the real component, internally consistent and capable of confirming the agreed execution.

The country of origin is not the central risk factor. The same gap can arise in domestic fabrication when responsibilities are unclear or documents are merely ticked off formally.

Brief standards context

DIN EN 1090-2:2024-09 provides technical execution rules for steel structures, including welding planning, qualification, fabrication, inspection and testing. Release therefore depends on the combined evidence, not a single certificate.

DIN EN ISO 17635:2025-08 provides general rules for non-destructive testing of welds and supports the selection of suitable methods, techniques and acceptance levels. An inspection report is meaningful only together with a clearly defined test object and inspection scope.

What must be resolved before erection

  1. Which requirements apply to the specific connection?
  2. Do the WPS, procedure qualification and personnel qualifications cover the actual work?
  3. Are fabrication and inspections uniquely traceable to the component and relevant welds?
  4. Who assessed deviations and accepted conformity responsibly?

The Kew case shows why the final question is decisive. If procurement, project management, the fabricator, the inspection body and erection each review only part of the file, a seemingly complete package can conceal non-conforming fabrication.

The decisive release statement is not: “The documents are complete.” It is: “The documents were technically reviewed, match the specific execution and confirm that the component was fabricated and inspected in accordance with the requirements.”

How the damage could have been prevented

Before erection, a present-day component-specific conformity review would have linked the design drawings, fabrication requirements, welding procedures, personnel qualifications, inspection plan and reports to the actual GT1 girder delivered. Independent welding coordination should also have confirmed that visual and ultrasonic examinations were uniquely assigned to the load-bearing butt welds and that their findings represented the real fabrication. The girder should not have been released for erection without written confirmation of conformity.

Critical decision point
Acceptance and erection release of the imported GT1 girder.
Suitable present-day method
Independent component-specific welding QA review with full traceability and targeted visual and ultrasonic examination of load-bearing welds.
Finding that would become visible
Missing or unreliable consistency between drawing, fabrication requirements, WPS, welder qualifications, inspection extent, reports and actual execution.
Required action
Quarantine the girder and do not erect it; verify the actual fabrication completely, assess deviations competently and repair, replace or release the girder only after written evidence.
Limit
A complete documentation package and sample inspections prove the real execution only when component, weld identity and inspection scope are uniquely traceable. Non-destructive testing also has method- and access-dependent detection limits.
